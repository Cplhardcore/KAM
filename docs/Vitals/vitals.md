Vitals

Updating the Vitals Loop: 
The vitals loop now fully incorporates a number of vital calculations, such as SpO2, into the ACE vitals loop rather than treating them as independent. Because of this, we can now produce a much more dynamic range of patient presentations to enhance gameplay, rewarding knowledge and respect for the interconnectedness of the human body. In order to do this, we have had to introduce a number of additional systems which work to push and pull each other to maintain homeostasis in a variety of conditions. These systems are as follows:

V/Q Matching

A healthy human body matches ventilation (the volume of air inspired, V) with perfusion (the amount of blood entering the lungs to conduct exchange, Q) at a ratio of 0.8. In our model, this ratio increases as blood is lost to simulate anaerobic respiration to a peak of 1.2(*1) which occurs around 1.5 liters of blood loss. Main takeaway for this is that as Q increases (from increased heart rate), V will also increase, and vice versa. 

In order for V to match Q, the body will either increase or decrease the respiratory rate depending on whether V is not enough or too much respectively. However, if V cannot match demand at the set V/Q ratio for the amount of blood lost, you will start seeing impacts on PaCO2 and PaO2 which can cause cascading issues down the line. If V is short of Q, then PaCO2 will increase and PaO2 will decrease but if V is greater than Q, PaCO2 will decrease and PaO2 will increase. It is also important to note that V is determined by multiplying respiratory rate by tidal volume, which decreases or becomes nonexistent in cases of PTX/TPTX/HPTX and edema. So as tidal volume decreases, respiratory rate will need to increase to maintain the same level of ventilation. 

Blood Gas Tension


We talked about two values in the last section, PaCO2 and PaO2. These values, along with a few others, make up our group of blood gas tension values which are important for our vitals loop. PaCO2 and PaO2 are partial pressure values that measure the pressure generated by CO2 and O2 in the blood; increases in these values indicate increased presence in the blood. These are important values to understand because they impact other systems in critical ways. 

PaCO2 primary works within our buffer simulation and is the second greatest contributor to acidosis (the first one being saline). As previously discussed, when ventilation is unable to meet cardiac demand, there will be a decreased exchange rate in the lungs, resulting in an accumulation of PaCO2 in the bloodstream. The body can limit this accumulation, but slowly becomes less effective at it as blood is lost. PaCO2 can be approximated by ETCO2 which will always be a few points short of PaCO2, anywhere between 3 to 10 mmHg depending on how high PaCO2 is. Normal range for PaCO2 is 35-45 mmHg.

PaO2 is our direct link to SpO2 which is one of the critical vitals that can result in unconsciousness, cardiac arrest, or death. Just like PaCO2, PaO2 primarily changes when ventilation is unable to meet cardiac demand, just in the opposite direction. Decreased exchange ratios result in a decreased PaO2 while increased exchange ratios result in an increase. However, this movement is mediated by PAO2 which is the O2 pressure in the alveoli, the sacks which enable O2 exchange in the lungs. PAO2 is calculated with inputs from atmospheric pressure and the percentage of oxygen inspired (FiO2) with both having a positive linear relationship. As pressure decreases due to an increase in altitude, PAO2 decreases. As FiO2 increases due to the patient being placed on 100% O2, PAO2 increases. FiO2 can also drop to zero if there is an airway collapse. The important thing to note is that PaO2 moves slowly but will be driven in the direction of PAO2, if PAO2 is zero because of an airway issue, then PaO2 will slowly move towards zero, but if PAO2 is high due to the patient being placed on O2, then PaO2 will slowly increase towards whatever value PAO2 is at. Note this is not exactly 1-to-1, similar to ETCO2 and PaCO2, PaO2 and PAO2 will have a difference which increases as PAO2 increases. If PAO2 is 100 mmHg, PaO2 will be around 90 mmHg, if PAO2 is 500 mmHg (like with 100% O2), then PaO2 should end up around 150 mmHg. Normal range for PaO2 is 85-95 mmHg. 


This brings us to SpO2 which is the percentage of fully saturated hemoglobin in the blood. SpO2 is directly calculated from PaO2 through the oxygen dissociation curve which is a sigmoidal function. This function can shift either left or right primarily due to pH changes with temperature playing secondary effects. Right shifts come from a decreased pH or increased temperature, left shifts come from the opposite. These shifts are important to understand because it means that even if PaO2 is constant, SpO2 can change under it resulting in unconsciousness, cardiac arrest, or death. This also means that at certain points along the curve, small shifts in PaO2 can result in large shifts in SpO2. Normal range for SpO2 is 95-97%.

Internal Temperature


The body will attempt to maintain an internal temperature of 37 degrees celsius. This internal system is actively working against environmental temperature which decreases due to map latitude, altitude, and time of day. This system can also be hampered by loss of blood volume, and can actively be harmed by the infusion of cold or even ambient temperature fluids. To counter these effects, we have two tools at our disposal to help support patient temperature, those being hand warmers and fluid warmers. Hand warmers can be on each limb and the chest to improve heat generation, with each warmer lasting for one hour before losing effectiveness. Medics can place fluid warmers on active IV lines and warm those incoming fluids to greatly increase heat generation ability in the short term. Hand warmers are fully consumed after use, fluid warmers can be reused.

Ph Buffering 


As previously discussed, pH can be decreased or increased based on the amount of PaCO2 and HCO3 in the blood. PaCO2 is controlled by the lungs and HCO3 is controlled by the kidneys. The kidneys also are responsible for removing H+ in the blood from acidic fluids like saline and will slowly remove these hydrogen ions, but can become overwhelmed and shut down if the blood becomes too acidic or too much saline is pushed in a short timeframe, harming both the removal of H+ and removal of HCO3, resulting in metabolic acidosis. These damages can be undone by manually correcting the pH imbalance, either through the respiratory system or through dialysis. 

Response-Driven Fluid Shift and Vasoconstriction


As fluids are lost, the body will begin a number of compensatory mechanisms to support fluid volumes and reduce loss. One of these compensatory mechanisms is the mobilization of fluids in the interstitial space into the bloodstream or extracellular space*. The interstitial space contains ten liters of fluid which can be recruited to support plasma loss with an additional half liter of red blood cells in the spleen, meaning that initial blood loss will actually be quite slow as these efforts work to maintain actual blood volume. Adding to this is vasoconstriction which starts increasing in response to volume changes.

However, eventually these efforts will begin to fail, normally as the spleen RBCs run out and fluid volumes will begin decreasing. However, if the blood loss is stopped, the recruitment of interstitial fluid will continue until the patient is at a 60% gradient between the extracellular and interstitial space, meaning that patient blood volumes will actually increase after blood loss is stopped. After all volume loss has stopped and this is at a normal range, the body will slowly begin to recover the lost interstitial space fluids and spleen RBCs, but these efforts are slow and can be greatly assisted with additional fluid deliveries above what would be required to get the patient back to 6 liters of blood.
